A Power Not Granted Does Not Exist

The Constitution is an enumerated powers document. The Federal government holds only the powers We the People granted it. The burden of proof falls on the power claimed — never on the citizen who denies it.

In transportation, two powers were enumerated, and only two:

  • "post Roads" (Article I, Section 8) — delivering letters, in defense of the Federal obligation for free speech.
  • "No Preference" (Article I, Section 9) — no preference in the facilities of commerce of one State over another.

Everything else — "internal improvements" — was reserved to the States and the people.

"The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite. The former will be exercised principally on external objects, as war, peace, negotiation, and foreign commerce; with which last the power of taxation will, for the most part, be connected. The powers reserved to the several States will extend to all the objects which, in the ordinary course of affairs, concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people, and the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the State."

James Madison, Federalist #45
1773

The Tea Party Fought a Mercantile Monopoly

The Tea Act of May 1773 granted the East India Company the right to ship its tea directly to the colonies and sell it through its own appointed consignees — a government-granted monopoly over the supply chain of a staple of colonial commerce, bypassing colonial merchants and shippers.

On December 16, 1773, Bostonians dumped 342 chests of the Company's tea into the harbor. Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts. Within two years, Americans were at war.

The Boston Tea Party was a demonstration against a general government's mercantile monopoly that triggered a war. Fourteen years later, the men who wrote the Constitution had not forgotten it.

September 14, 1787

The Framers Refused, 8 States to 3

Four days before the Convention adjourned, Dr. Franklin moved to expand the "post Roads" power. Madison moved to expand it further. Rufus King and George Mason named the danger: mercantile monopolies.

"Docr. FRANKLIN moved to add after the words "post roads" Art I. Sect. 8. "a power to provide for cutting canals where deemed necessary""

Madison's Notes of Debates, September 14, 1787

"The expence in such cases will fall on the U. States, and the benefit accrue to the places where the canals may be cut."

Roger Sherman (Connecticut)

Madison moved to enlarge the motion into a power "to grant charters of incorporation where the interest of the U.S. might require & the legislative provisions of individual States may be incompetent." His object was "to secure an easy communication between the States."

"The States will be prejudiced and divided into parties by it. In Philada. & New York, It will be referred to the establishment of a Bank, which has been a subject of contention in those Cities. In other places it will be referred to mercantile monopolies."

Rufus King (Massachusetts)

James Wilson replied that "As to mercantile monopolies they are already included in the power to regulate trade." Mason rejected that:

"Col: MASON was for limiting the power to the single case of Canals. He was afraid of monopolies of every sort, which he did not think were by any means already implied by the Constitution as supposed by Mr. Wilson."

George Mason (Virginia)

Even limited to canals alone, the power was refused:

February 6, 1792

"All Roads Other Than Post Roads"

Five years later, Representative James Madison opposed a bill paying bounties to the cod fisheries. His warning names the line exactly: post roads are Federal; every other road is not.

"those who ratified the constitution conceived, that this is not an indefinite government deriving its powers from the general terms prefixed to the specified powers—but, a limited government tied down to the specified powers, which explain and define the general terms."

James Madison, House of Representatives, Bounty Payments for Cod Fisheries

"If Congress can apply money indefinitely to the general welfare, and are the sole and supreme judges of the general welfare, they may take the care of religion into their own hands; they may establish teachers in every state, county, and parish, and pay them out of the public treasury; they may take into their own hands the education of children, establishing in like manner schools throughout the union; they may assume the provision for the poor; they may undertake the regulation of all roads other than post roads; in short, every thing, from the highest object of state legislation, down to the most minute object of police, would be thrown under the power of Congress"

James Madison, February 6, 1792 · postRoads.com/1792madison

No Preference in the Facilities of Commerce

"No Preference shall be given by any Regulation of Commerce or Revenue to the Ports of one State over those of another"

U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 9

Taxing the whole nation to build the facilities of commerce of favored places is a preference. Senator Henry Clay spent a career trying to fund roads in Kentucky with Federal money; President Jackson's Maysville Road veto stopped him.

When the Confederate States wrote their constitution in 1861, they made the limit explicit. It carries no legal weight — but it shows how the drafters of the time read the intent of the Framers:

"To regulate commerce with foreign nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian tribes; but neither this, nor any other clause contained in the Constitution, shall ever be construed to delegate the power to Congress to appropriate money for any internal improvement intended to facilitate commerce; except for the purpose of furnishing lights, beacons, and buoys, and other aids to navigation upon the coasts, and the improvement of harbors and the removing of obstructions in river navigation; in all which cases such duties shall be laid on the navigation facilitated thereby as may be necessary to pay the costs and expenses thereof."

Constitution of the Confederate States, March 11, 1861, Article I, Section 8, Clause 3 · postRoads.com · No Preference
1817 – 1867

21 Vetoes Held the Line

For half a century, Presidents Madison, Monroe, Jackson, Tyler, Polk, Pierce, Buchanan, and Johnson enforced the "post Roads" limit with veto messages against Federal taxing to build internal improvements.

"The legislative powers vested in Congress are specified and enumerated in the eighth section of the first article of the Constitution, and it does not appear that the power proposed to be exercised by the bill is among the enumerated powers"

President Madison, Bonus Bill veto, March 3, 1817 — a bill to fund "constructing roads and canals"

"Among the powers expressly granted to Congress by the Constitution, there is no one which authorizes appropriations from the Treasury for internal improvements, for the purposes of commerce or otherwise, within the limits of a State."

President Buchanan, 1859

"The Constitution does not expressly give Congress the power to make appropriations for purposes of this kind, and I am not aware that it possesses such power unless by implication."

President Andrew Johnson, 1867
July 11, 1916

The Monopoly Rebuilt

The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 ignored the Convention, Madison, and 21 veto messages. Federal taxing to build roads began, and transportation became a Top-down Federal plan built around the 25 mpg efficiency of the Model-T.

The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 scaled it. It was not a Left or Right project: Senator Albert Gore Sr. wrote it; President Eisenhower signed it. The Left wanted Federal spending; the Right wanted the oil-powered mercantile economy. Both used the same tax collectors, and both ignored the enumerated limit.

King warned the power would be "referred to mercantile monopolies." It was. The government that holds the war-power took up commercial self-interest — the same mixing the Tea Party fought in 1773. A single Federal design — two tons of car to move one person — became mandatory for American cities. The 1975 Congressional study PB-244854, Automated Guideway Transit, found "institutional failures" had blocked transportation innovation for "four to six decades."

Three Mercantile Monopolies

A mercantile monopoly is Federal control of the means of production: the war and police powers of government mixed with commerce. Three examples — mobility, labor, and energy.

1. Mobility: Federal Highways

In 1902, before government transport monopolies, every US city over 10,000 people had a privately funded streetcar system providing mobility as a service. Federal highways wiped them out, and replaced 45% of freight railroads with roads — despite railroads' 188 times greater efficiency (470 ton-mpg, versus a 200 pound person at 25 mpg).

The highway monopoly created foreign oil addiction, and oil wars since 1990 to secure those supplies. Since the US military shifted from rail to trucks, its war record is 0 wins and 3 losses.

2. Labor: Fugitive Slave Laws

The Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850 put Federal police power behind the control of labor inputs into commerce — a mercantile monopoly on the employment of labor. The 1850 Act commanded citizens to assist in capture and denied the accused a jury.

Northern states answered with personal liberty laws — nullification. South Carolina's 1860 Declaration of Causes cited that nullification as a cause of secession. Illicit Energy built the path to the Civil War.

3. Energy: Rural Electrification Act, 1936

The Rural Electrification Act of 1936 favored centralized, coal-burning power monopolies. It wiped out a Bottom-up wind industry that was making 80,000 windmills a year; about 600,000 windmills were abandoned.

Renewables are recovering only now, Bottom-up: wind supplies 62% of electricity in Iowa, 34% in Oklahoma, 24% in Texas.

States Are Sovereign. States Forbid Monopolies.

The Remedy: Restore Free Markets

The path has been walked before. A 1918 Federal communications monopoly held back the technologies of the Internet for most of a century. In 1982 the courts restored free markets, and the Internet followed. Transportation can be restored the same way — Bottom-up, State by State, city by city.

The 5X5 Standard

Cities and States, sovereign over internal improvements, grant rights-of-way to transportation networks that:

  1. Are privately funded.
  2. Operate without government subsidies.
  3. Exceed 5 times the efficiency of existing roads (125 mpg or equivalent).
  4. Pay 5 percent of gross transportation revenues to the rights-of-way holders.
  5. Exceed the safety performance of modes already approved for use in the rights-of-way.

"What prudent merchant will hazard his fortunes in any new branch of commerce when he knows not but that his plans may be rendered unlawful before they can be executed?"

James Madison, Federalist #62
