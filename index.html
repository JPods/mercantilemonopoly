A Power Not Granted is Retained by the People

The Constitution is an enumerated powers document. Powers granted are limited to those explicitly granted.

  • "promote the general Welfare" The federal mission statement is the Preamble and is restated in Amendments 9 and 10 of the Bill of Rights. The Preamble enumerates a restriction on federal authorities to only "promote the general welfare." We the People are sovereign over the "general welfare." Two aspects of our liberty intertwine to innovate the general welfare: tolerance of Disruptive Minorities offering choices (inventors) and tolerance of the Wisdom of the Many sorting those choices in free markets and free speech.
  • "post Roads" (Article I, Section 8) — delivering letters, in defense of the Federal obligation for free speech.
  • "No Preference" (Article I, Section 9) — no preference in the facilities of commerce of one State over another.

Everything else — "internal improvements" — was reserved to the States and the people.

"The powers delegated by the proposed Constitution to the federal government are few and defined. Those which are to remain in the State governments are numerous and indefinite. The former will be exercised principally on external objects, as war, peace, negotiation, and foreign commerce; with which last the power of taxation will, for the most part, be connected. The powers reserved to the several States will extend to all the objects which, in the ordinary course of affairs, concern the lives, liberties, and properties of the people, and the internal order, improvement, and prosperity of the State."

James Madison, Federalist #45
1773

The Boston Tea Party Confronted a Mercantile Monopoly

The Tea Act of May 1773 granted the East India Company the right to ship its tea directly to the colonies and sell it through its own appointed consignees — a government-granted monopoly over the supply chain of a staple of colonial commerce, bypassing colonial merchants and shippers.

On December 16, 1773, Bostonians dumped 342 chests of the Company's tea into the harbor. Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts. Within two years, Americans were at war.

The Boston Tea Party was a demonstration against a general government's mercantile monopoly that triggered a war. Fourteen years later, the men who wrote the Constitution had not forgotten it.

Taxing the whole nation to build the facilities of commerce of favored places is a preference. Senator Henry Clay spent a career trying to fund roads in Kentucky with Federal money; President Jackson's Maysville Road veto stopped him.

When the Confederate States wrote their constitution in 1861, they made the limit explicit. It carries no legal weight — but it shows how the drafters of the time read the intent of the Framers:

July 11, 1916

The Monopoly Rebuilt

The Federal Aid Road Act of 1916 ignored the Convention, Madison, and 21 veto messages. Federal taxing to build roads began, and transportation became a Top-down Federal plan built around the 25 mpg efficiency of the Model-T.

What the monopoly replaced was working. In 1902, every US city over 10,000 people had one or more privately funded streetcar systems providing mobility as a service. The highway monopoly wiped them out.

In 1916, US railroads reached their peak of about 254,000 miles. Railroads move a ton about 470 miles per gallon (Warren Buffett on rail efficiency) — 188 times the efficiency of a car moving a 200-pound person at 25 mpg. The highway monopoly wiped out about 45% of those railroads.

The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 scaled it. It was not a Left or Right project: Senator Albert Gore Sr. wrote it; President Eisenhower signed it. The Left wanted Federal spending; the Right wanted the oil-powered mercantile economy. Both used the same tax collectors, and both ignored the enumerated limit.

King warned the power would be "referred to mercantile monopolies." It was. The government that holds the war-power took up commercial self-interest — the same mixing the Tea Party fought in 1773. A single Federal design — two tons of car to move one person — became mandatory for American cities. The 1975 Congressional study PB-244854, Automated Guideway Transit, found "institutional failures" had blocked transportation innovation for "four to six decades."

Open for Review

Two ideas on this page need review by people who can test them: the concept of Disposable Energy, and the correlation of oil imports and oil wars with national debt. We cannot do everything. We need academics to contribute.

Disposable Energy is how much energy people can buy with their take-home pay. GDP measures the speed of the economic flywheel; Disposable Energy measures the input to it — labor applying affordable energy — which shows up in outcomes only after a time delay.

Measured in actual dollars — take-home pay and the gas price in the same month — so inflation cancels in the ratio. First results from public BLS, BEA, and EIA data: a rise in gasoline prices is followed by rising unemployment about 18 months later, and a change in Disposable Energy leads real GDP growth by about one year. A week's take-home pay bought more gasoline in 1973 than in 2008, 2012, or 2022. The correlations are modest, and the post-2005 separation of debt from oil imports is not yet explained.

The Remedy: Restore Free Markets

The path has been walked before. A 1918 Federal communications monopoly held back the technologies of the Internet for most of a century. In 1982 the courts restored free markets, and the Internet followed. Transportation can be restored the same way — Bottom-up, State by State, city by city.

The 5X5 Standard

Cities and States, sovereign over internal improvements, grant rights-of-way to transportation networks that:

  1. Are privately funded.
  2. Operate without government subsidies.
  3. Exceed 5 times the efficiency of existing roads (125 mpg or equivalent).
  4. Pay 5 percent of gross transportation revenues to the rights-of-way holders.
  5. Exceed the safety performance of modes already approved for use in the rights-of-way.
